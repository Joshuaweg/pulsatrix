    Recipe: Is the Student Born Better?¶

    What you'll build: born-again networks (Furlanello et al., ICML 2018) on MNIST, with the controls their claim needs. A network is trained on the labels (generation 0). A second network of the same size is trained on the first one's outputs (generation 1), a third on the second's, and a fourth on the third's. Furlanello et al. found students that beat their teachers, and an ensemble of the generations that did better still. The recipe repeats this over five seeds, against three controls:

    • another network trained on the labels from another start, so a gain can be set against the spread between two runs that differ only in their seed;
    • the permuted teacher (Furlanello et al.'s DKPP): generation 1 again, from the same start and with the same batches, but taught by generation 0's outputs with every class except the top one shuffled. It keeps the teacher's answer and confidence and scrambles what the teacher says about the other classes;
    • an ensemble of three networks trained on the labels, the fair comparison for the ensemble of the three born-again generations.

    Accuracy can't say whether a student uses its teacher's evidence. So the recipe also compares explanations, with the test from KD-2: saliency maps of the true class on 200 test digits, from generation 1 and generation 0, and from the other label network and generation 0.

    The predictions were written down before the first run:

    1. Generation 1 is more accurate than generation 0 on average, by less than one percentage point, and not on every seed.
    2. The permuted teacher's student is less accurate than generation 1: the gain comes from what the teacher says about the wrong classes.
    3. The ensemble of three label networks is at least as accurate as the ensemble of three born-again generations, which learned from one another and so make more of the same errors.
    4. Generation 1's explanations agree with generation 0's more than the other label network's do, on every seed.

    Only prediction 3 holds. Generation 1 is no better than its teacher. The permuted teacher's student is better than generation 1 on every seed. And generation 1's explanations are no closer to its teacher's than an independent network's. The teacher is 99% sure on the images it trained on, so a student trained to match it at T = 1 is close to being trained on the labels again. A follow-up with a softer teacher (T = 4) separates the two questions: the student copies more of its teacher's evidence, and is less accurate for it.

    CMake target: born_again_networks_recipe (examples/recipes/born_again_networks.cpp).

    ./build/born_again_networks_recipe MNIST_DIR out/ [--device cpu|hip] [--seeds 5] [--train 10000] \
    [--epochs 10] [--generations 3] [--temperature 1] [--smoke]

    Note

    The recipe needs MNIST's four IDX files in MNIST_DIR (tools/fetch_mnist.py downloads them). It runs on the CPU in a few minutes. --smoke runs on made-up data in a few seconds to check the plumbing; its numbers mean nothing.

    Code¶

    Each network is LeNet-300-100 (784 → 300 → 100 → 10, with ReLUs), trained with Adam for 10 epochs on the first 10,000 training images. Generation 0 learns from the labels, and each later generation from its teacher's logits, precomputed on every training image:

    DistillationOptions dopt;
dopt.temperature = o.temperature;           // 1: Furlanello et al.'s choice
DistillationLoss soft(backend, dopt);       // no labels: alpha doesn't apply
(void)soft.forward(net.seq.forward(x), teacher_logits_of_the_batch);
(void)net.seq.backward(soft.backward());

    The permuted teacher is the same loop with the teacher's logits shuffled again each epoch:

    targets = PermuteDarkKnowledge(teacher_logits, seed * 1000 + epoch, &cpu);

    The ensembles average the members' probabilities:

    const Tensor ensemble = EnsembleLogProbabilities({gen1_logits, gen2_logits, gen3_logits}, &cpu);

    Each explanation is the gradient of the true class with respect to the pixels. Two networks' maps of the same 200 digits are compared with CompareExplanations (KD-2), whose permutation test asks whether the agreement is about these digits or just the general shape of a saliency map.

    What's happening¶

    Prediction Result
    1 Generation 1 beats generation 0, by under a point, not on every seed Fails: −0.04 points, better on 1 of 5 seeds
    2 The permuted teacher's student is worse than generation 1 Fails: it is better, by 0.14 points, on 5 of 5 seeds
    3 Three label networks ensemble at least as well as three generations Holds: +0.27 points, on 5 of 5 seeds
    4 Generation 1's explanations are closer to its teacher's Fails: closer on 2 of 5 seeds, by at most 0.008

    No born-again gain. Generation 1 is 0.04 points below generation 0, and two networks trained on the labels from different starts differ by 0.14 points (standard deviation). Later generations drift down a little, to 95.51%. The teacher is the likely reason: on the images it trained on, its mean top probability is 99.07%. At T = 1 its outputs are nearly the labels, with about 1% of the probability left to say anything about the other classes, so the student is trained on the labels again, from a different start.

    The scrambled teacher did better than the real one, on every seed (a sign test gives p = 1/32 ≈ 0.03 for five of five). Shuffling keeps each row's values, so its targets are exactly as confident as the real teacher's; only which wrong classes get the leftover probability changes. One reading, not tested here: the real teacher's leftover probability repeats its own confusions, which its students then copy, while shuffled probability is noise that regularizes. Either way, this run's gain over the labels didn't come from the dark knowledge.

    The ensembles agree with prediction 3. Three networks trained on the labels from different starts ensemble to 96.01%, and three born-again generations to 95.73%. That fits generations that copy one another and so make more of the same errors, though the recipe doesn't measure the overlap directly. The born-again ensemble is no better than the single permuted-teacher student.

    The explanations agree with the accuracy. Every pair of maps agrees far more than maps of different digits do (p = 0.001, the smallest 999 permutations can give), and generation 1 agrees with its teacher no more than an independent network does: Spearman 0.38 to 0.41 for both. A student trained on near-labels didn't learn its teacher's evidence any more than a fresh network learns it from the labels alone.

    A follow-up: a softer teacher¶

    The first run points at the temperature: at T = 1 the teacher has almost nothing to say beyond the labels. --temperature 4 softens both distributions, so the teacher's mean top probability on its training images drops from 99.07% to 71.72%. This run was designed after seeing the first, so it follows up on it and isn't part of its test. Its predictions were written down before it ran: (a) generation 1 is more accurate than generation 0 on average, and (b) generation 1's explanations agree with its teacher's more than the other label network's do, on at least 4 of 5 seeds.

    Test accuracy, mean ± sd over 5 seeds:

  generation 1                      95.30% ± 0.15
  generation 2                      95.05% ± 0.08
  generation 3                      94.85% ± 0.07
  another network on the labels     95.62% ± 0.12
  student of the permuted teacher   95.67% ± 0.10
  ensemble of generations 1-3       95.12% ± 0.14
  ensemble of 3 label networks      96.01% ± 0.05

Generation 0's mean top probability on its training images, at T = 4.0: 71.72%

    • (a) fails, and more clearly than before. Generation 1 is 0.33 points below its teacher, on every seed, and each generation loses more: 95.30%, 95.05%, 94.85%. The born-again ensemble falls to 95.12%, 0.89 points below the label networks' ensemble.
    • (b) holds. Generation 1's saliency maps agree with its teacher's at Spearman 0.45 to 0.46, on every seed, against 0.38 to 0.41 for the independent network.

    So the softer teacher passes on more of its evidence, and the student is less accurate for it. Agreement between explanations measures how faithfully the student copies the teacher, not whether the copy is any good. A student can agree more with a teacher's explanations and make more mistakes. This also fits the T = 1 run: with nothing to pass on but the labels, the student matched the teacher no better than a stranger did.

    A measure that couldn't fail¶

    The first version of the recipe compared gradient × input maps, the usual choice. Every comparison came out at Spearman 0.995: generation 1 against its teacher and the independent network against it, on every seed. Gradient × input is zero wherever the pixel is zero, and most of an MNIST digit is blank background, the same blank pixels in both maps. Two maps that share most of their zeros have almost the same ranks whatever the networks do. Saliency, the gradient alone, has no such shared zeros. Before trusting an agreement score, check that two maps you expect to differ can come out different.
