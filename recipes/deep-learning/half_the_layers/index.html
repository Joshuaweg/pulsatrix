    Recipe: Half the Layers¶

    What you'll build: a student language model half the depth of its teacher, made the way DistilBERT was (Sanh et al., 2019). SmolLM2-135M-Instruct has 30 blocks. The student keeps blocks 0, 2, 4, ..., 28, starts from their weights, and trains on chat text with DistilBERT's three losses: the teacher's softened outputs, the next token, and a cosine loss pulling the student's last hidden states toward the teacher's. Five students, the same 300 steps on the same batches:

    • untrained: the 15 kept blocks as they are;
    • language modeling: next-token cross-entropy alone;
    • distilled: DistilBERT's 5 : 2 : 1 mix of distillation (T = 2), cross-entropy and cosine;
    • distilled without the cosine loss: 5 : 2;
    • distilled from random blocks: the 5 : 2 : 1 mix, with every block's weight matrices redrawn at random (each at its own scale). The embedding and the norms are still the teacher's.

    Then a question the loss can't answer: does the distilled student predict the next token from the same tokens as the teacher? AttnLRP token relevance for the teacher's top next token, compared between teacher and student with KD-2's test.

    The predictions were written down before the first run:

    1. Half the layers, untrained, breaks the model: held-out perplexity more than 10 times the teacher's.
    2. Distillation beats language modeling alone: lower held-out perplexity and more of the teacher's top-1 next tokens.
    3. Starting from the teacher's blocks matters more than the loss: the student from random blocks ends with more than twice the language-modeling student's perplexity.
    4. The cosine loss matters little here: dropping it moves held-out perplexity by less than 5%.
    5. The distilled student's token relevance agrees with the teacher's more than the language-modeling student's does (mean Spearman).

    Predictions 1, 3 and 4 hold; 2 and 5 fail. Distillation makes the student's outputs much more like the teacher's (a third of the KL divergence) without making its explanations any more like the teacher's. And the headline perplexity hides two different things, which the recipe separates.

    CMake target: half_the_layers_recipe (examples/recipes/half_the_layers.cpp).

    ./build/half_the_layers_recipe MODEL_DIR out/ --text conversations.jsonl [--device hip] [--steps 300]

    Note

    The recipe needs HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct (270 MB): model.safetensors, config.json and tokenizer.json in one directory, and a JSONL file of chat conversations from tools/explain/fetch_chat_sample.py. On a GPU a run takes about 15 minutes. --smoke runs the tiny test model (tests/fixtures/hf_tiny/llama) on a few built-in sentences to check the plumbing; its numbers mean nothing.

    Code¶

    The text is cut into windows of 128 tokens. Whole conversations go to training (the first 90%) or to the held-out set, so none is in both. The student, and taps that give the cosine loss its gradient:

    std::unique_ptr<CausalLM> student = MakeStudentFromLayers(*teacher, EveryOtherLayer(30), backend);
student->set_intervention(14, std::make_unique<HiddenStateTap>(backend));
teacher->set_intervention(29, std::make_unique<HiddenStateTap>(backend));

    One distillation step, with DistilBERT's weights: DistillationLoss with alpha = 5/7 mixes the soft and hard terms 5 : 2, and the cosine loss enters at 1/7:

    DistillationOptions dopt;
dopt.temperature = 2.0f;
dopt.alpha = 5.0f / 7.0f;
DistillationLoss kd(backend, dopt);

const Tensor logits = student->forward(ids);
float loss = kd.forward(logits, teacher->forward(ids), targets);
loss += cos.forward(tap->hidden(), teacher_tap->hidden()) / 7.0f;
Tensor g = cos.backward();
backend->axpby(1.0f / 7.0f, g.data(), 0.0f, g.data(), g.data(), static_cast<size_t>(g.numel()));
tap->add_gradient(g);
student->backward(kd.backward());
ClipGradNorm(*student, 1.0f);
opt.step(*student);                                  // AdamW, lr 2e-4

    The explanations are DiffRelevance(teacher, student, ids, target, LxtAttnLrpConfig()) on 24 held-out prompts of 32 tokens, with the target fixed to the teacher's top next token, so both models explain the same prediction.

    What's happening¶

    Prediction Result
    1 Untrained, perplexity over 10 times the teacher's Holds: 234,066 times
    2 Distilled beats language modeling on perplexity and the teacher's top-1 Fails: perplexity 4.60 against 4.55; top-1 63.8% against 61.1%
    3 Random blocks end over twice as perplexed as language modeling Holds: 3.3 times
    4 Dropping the cosine loss moves perplexity under 5% Holds: 0.2%
    5 Distilled relevance closer to the teacher's than language modeling's Fails: Spearman 0.624 against 0.657; closer on 7 of 24 prompts

    Taking out every other block breaks the model completely. The untrained student's perplexity is over a million, and it picks the teacher's top token 0.2% of the time. The blocks that are left were never trained to follow one another. Yet 300 steps, about one pass over the training text, turn every student that started from them back into a working model: perplexity 6.15 or less on the text after the opening, against the teacher's 2.94.

    Students "beat" the teacher, and that isn't what it looks like. Every trained student but one has a lower held-out perplexity than the teacher: 4.55 against 6.08. All 2,379 conversations open with the same 24 tokens, a system prompt naming the assistant, and 59 of the 64 held-out windows start with them. The teacher predicts these tokens badly (perplexity 229.87), and the students learn them within a few hundred steps (1.00 to 1.49). On the rest of each window, the teacher is far better than any student: 2.94 against 5.78 at best. The split was added after the first run showed the students ahead of their teacher. It explains a number; it isn't part of a test.

    So prediction 2 fails on the number it named, and the split shows why. On the rest of the text the distilled student is better than the language-modeling one (5.78 against 6.15), picks the teacher's top token more often (63.8% against 61.1%), and is three times closer to the teacher's whole distribution (KL 0.90 against 2.81). On the opening it is worse (1.48 against 1.00), because it is partly trained toward a teacher that predicts the opening badly. The opening is a fifth of the tokens in most windows, enough to tip the total.

    The teacher's weights matter more than any loss. From random blocks, with the same losses and steps, the student ends at 15.05, and at 23.94 on the rest of the text. The cosine loss doesn't matter here: without it, perplexity moves 0.2%, and the top-1 agreement is even a little higher (65.1%).

    The explanations don't follow the outputs. Both trained students explain their predictions much like the teacher: Spearman 0.62 and 0.66, where the untrained student is at 0.14 and no better than its maps of other prompts (p = 0.877). But distillation doesn't bring the student's relevance closer to the teacher's. The language-modeling student is closer on Spearman and on the top 25% of tokens, and the distilled student is the closer one on only 7 of the 24 prompts. Only CKA, which compares the pattern across prompts, favors the distilled student (0.601 against 0.453). Matching the teacher's output distribution three times more closely didn't make the student use the teacher's evidence more. The born-again recipe found the reverse: a student that copied more of its teacher's evidence and got less accurate. Output agreement and explanation agreement are separate things, and each has to be measured.

    Two instruments that needed fixing¶

    Both were found by reading the raw numbers of the first run, and both changes are in the code above.

    • Perplexity over everything. The first run printed only the overall perplexity, which put every trained student ahead of its teacher. Splitting off the shared opening showed why.
    • Explanation prompts that were all the same. The first run explained the first 32 tokens of each window, so most prompts were the same 24-token system prompt plus 8 more tokens. The language-modeling student's 24 per-prompt agreements took only 7 distinct values. On those near-copies, prediction 5 held (Spearman 0.610 against 0.582). With each prompt starting after the shared opening, it fails (0.624 against 0.657). The fix went against the prediction. Check that a test's inputs really differ before reading its result.
